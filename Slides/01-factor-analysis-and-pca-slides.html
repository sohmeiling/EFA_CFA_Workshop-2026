Why Terminology Matters

  • Factor analysis terminology gets used loosely — this is one of the most common sources of student confusion
  • Keep this glossary handy as a reference for the rest of the workshop

Key Terms: What You Measure vs What You Infer

  • Observed variable (indicator, item, manifest variable): something you actually measured — a survey item, test score, questionnaire response
  • Latent variable: something you did not directly measure, but believe exists and drives the pattern of answers across observed variables — inferred only from correlations, never observed directly
  • Construct: the theoretical/conceptual idea a latent variable represents (e.g., “job satisfaction,” “maths anxiety”)
    • “Construct” and “latent variable” are often interchangeable; construct emphasises theoretical meaning, latent variable emphasises statistical role

Key Terms: Factor vs Component

  • Factor: the statistical term for a latent variable specifically within factor analysis
    • “The model has three factors” = three latent variables being estimated
  • Component: specific to PCA
    • A weighted combination of observed variables, built to capture as much variance as possible
    • Easy to confuse with “factor” — different logic entirely (see PCA section)
    • A component is built from observed variables; a factor is the underlying cause of observed variables

Key Terms: Loading, Communality, Uniqueness, Fit

  • Factor loading: how strongly an observed variable relates to a factor (higher absolute value = stronger relationship)
  • Communality: proportion of a variable’s variance explained by the factor(s) it loads on
  • Uniqueness (error variance): the leftover proportion not explained by the factor(s) — variable-specific, includes measurement error
  • Eigenvalue: how much total variance a possible factor/component accounts for — used to decide how many factors to keep
  • Model fit: how well a proposed structure reproduces the observed correlation pattern — central to CFA, relevant to EFA too

Putting the Terms Together

  • Observed variables = what you measure
  • Latent variable / construct / factor = near-synonyms; the unobserved thing believed to cause the pattern in what you measured
  • Loadings, communalities, uniqueness = describe the relationship between the two
  • Component = a related but conceptually different idea belonging to PCA, not factor analysis proper

What Is Factor Analysis?

Factor Analysis: The Big Idea

  • A family of statistical methods for identifying underlying relationships between measured variables
  • Core idea: if observed variables correlate, that correlation may be explained by one or more latent variables (factors) we cannot measure directly
  • Purpose: reduce many observed variables into a smaller number of latent factors that account for shared patterns of correlation

Two Branches (Plus a Close Relative)

  • Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA): used when the factor structure is not yet known — let the data suggest one
  • Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA): used when you already have a specific, theory-based structure to test
  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA): a closely related technique, often taught alongside FA and frequently confused with it — covered separately, first

FA and the Validity Framework

  • Validity evidence for a measurement instrument comes from five sources (AERA, APA, & NCME, 2014):
    1. Test content
    2. Response processes
    3. Internal structure
    4. Relationships to other variables
    5. Consequences of testing
  • FA (both EFA and CFA) is the primary tool for generating internal structure evidence (Arifin, 2023)
  • Reliability analysis is the other major internal-structure source, usually reported alongside FA:
    • Cronbach’s alpha — traditional, for EFA-derived factors
    • McDonald’s omega — increasingly preferred for CFA-derived factors (doesn’t assume every item contributes equally) (Brown, 2015)

Steps in Factor Analysis (General Overview)

  1. Data collection — adequate sample size for stable estimates
  2. Correlation matrix — the raw material FA works from
  3. Extraction — statistical method extracts candidate factors/components
  4. Rotation (EFA only) — simplifies the structure for interpretation
  5. Interpretation — examine loadings to understand what each factor represents
  6. Validation — assess reliability/validity, sometimes with an independent CFA

Applications of Factor Analysis

  • Psychology & education: underlying traits/constructs in personality inventories, attitude scales, assessments
  • Marketing: underlying dimensions of consumer preferences and behaviour
  • Health & social sciences: validating questionnaires and scales (wellbeing, quality of life, burnout)

General Assumptions of Factor Analysis

  • Adequate correlation among variables — checked via KMO and Bartlett’s test of sphericity
  • Linearity — relationships between observed variables and factor(s) assumed linear
  • Adequate sample size — rule of thumb: 5–10 observations per variable, minimum ~100–200 overall (depends heavily on loading strength and variables-per-factor)
  • No major outliers or extreme non-normality — both distort correlations and therefore the factor solution

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

What Is PCA?

  • Often taught alongside FA, frequently confused with it — but the two answer different questions
  • PCA reduces dimensionality by transforming observed variables into a smaller set of components: new variables that are weighted combinations of the originals
    • 1st component: captures as much total variance as possible
    • 2nd component: captures as much remaining variance as possible, and so on
  • PCA treats all variance in each variable — including measurement error and variable-specific quirks — as worth capturing

PCA vs Factor Analysis: The Core Difference

  • Factor analysis assumes genuine latent variables cause the correlations among observed variables
    • Explicitly separates communality (shared variance) from uniqueness (variable-specific variance, incl. error)
  • PCA builds new variables that summarise your data as efficiently as possible
  • Factor analysis tries to uncover the hidden causes that produced your data

When Does the Distinction Matter?

  • When variables are strongly correlated and communalities are high → PCA and FA often look very similar (part of why they get conflated)
  • The distinction matters most when:
    • Your goal is genuinely to identify underlying constructs for theory/measurement → favour FA
    • Your goal is purely data reduction / an efficient summary index or predictor → PCA is defensible and simpler
    • You plan to report “factor loadings” and interpret them as reflecting a latent construct → technically belongs to FA, not PCA (even though papers often blur this)

PCA Assumptions and Checks

  • PCA is a descriptive technique, not a formal statistical model — no distributional assumptions like ML-based EFA/CFA — but still has practical prerequisites:
    • Adequate correlation among variables (KMO, Bartlett’s)
    • Linearity — PCA finds linear combinations only
    • Sufficient sample size — small samples → unstable solutions
    • No extreme outliers — PCA maximises variance explained, so outliers can dominate the first component
    • Comparable variable scales, or standardisation — run on the correlation matrix (not covariance) if scales differ; this is Jamovi’s default

Testing PCA Assumptions in Jamovi

  • KMO: Factor → PCA → tick “KMO” under Assumption Checks. Overall KMO > ~.6 acceptable, > .8 good; also check individual-item KMO
  • Bartlett’s test of sphericity: same panel; significant (p < .05) supports using PCA
  • Correlation matrix inspection: tick “Correlation Matrix” to spot near-zero correlations
  • Outlier/normality check: Descriptives (histograms, boxplots) before running PCA

Steps to Run PCA in Jamovi

  1. Factor → Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
  2. Move all candidate variables into “Variables”
  3. Under Assumption Checks, tick KMO and Bartlett’s test — review before anything else
  4. Under Extraction, choose how many components to retain (eigenvalues > 1 is default; scree plot/parallel analysis often more defensible)
  5. Under Rotation, apply orthogonal rotation if helpful (Varimax) — PCA in Jamovi has no oblique option, that’s EFA-only
  6. Review eigenvalues, scree plot, component loadings (suppress loadings below e.g. .3)
  7. Save component scores back into the dataset if useful

Common Real-World Scenarios in PCA (1/2)

  1. KMO very low (< .5): variables aren’t correlated enough — reconsider variable set, remove poor items, or conclude PCA isn’t appropriate
  2. Bartlett’s test non-significant: correlation matrix ≈ “no correlation at all” — do not proceed as planned
  3. One variable loads weakly on every component: isn’t sharing variance — consider dropping, or check mis-coding/reverse-scoring

Common Real-World Scenarios in PCA (2/2)

  1. No clear elbow in the scree plot: run parallel analysis, or fall back on a theory-justified fixed number
  2. Nearly everything loads heavily on the first component: often a general “size/level” component — decide if that’s useful, or if it’s masking structure a multi-factor EFA would reveal
  3. Variables on very different scales: confirm PCA runs on the correlation matrix (Jamovi default), not covariance

Caution: PCA or EFA? A Decision Guide

The Real Question

  • One of the most common points of confusion for students meeting these methods for the first time
  • The useful question is not “which technique is more advanced?” but:

What will you do with the result afterwards?

Choose PCA When…

  • Your goal is pure data reduction, with no theoretical claim about a hidden cause
    • e.g., collapsing 20 correlated survey items into composite scores for a later regression/cluster analysis
  • You are not claiming the components represent “real” underlying constructs — you are compressing data efficiently

Choose EFA When…

  • Your goal is to make a claim about a latent construct
    • e.g., “these eight items are all measuring an underlying trait called maths anxiety”
  • EFA assumes a hidden variable causes your items to correlate, and tries to recover it
  • The standard approach for scale development and instrument validation in psychology, education, health research
    • (PCA is sometimes used there anyway, out of habit or software default)

Diagnostic and Follow-Up Rules

  • Check your communalities as a diagnostic:
    • High & uniform communalities → PCA and EFA tend to converge
    • Low or highly variable communalities → the two methods can diverge, because PCA still absorbs all variance (incl. item-specific noise) while EFA separates out just the shared part
  • If you plan to follow up with a CFA, use EFA (not PCA) at the exploratory stage
    • CFA tests a factor model; PCA components are not the same statistical object — a PCA-then-CFA pipeline is a mismatch, even though it appears in the literature

Rule of Thumb

  • Pure data reduction, no theoretical commitment → PCA
  • Uncovering/validating a latent construct, especially for publication or feeding into CFA → EFA

Key Points (Widaman, 2007) — 1/2

  • PCA reduces variables while retaining maximum information; EFA measures unobserved (latent), error-free variables
  • When observed variables share little in common: EFA fails to find a well-defined factor, but PCA will still find a well-defined component (a component is defined by maximising explained variance, not shared variance)
  • If the goal is to measure an error-free latent variable but PCA is used instead of EFA: loadings will typically be higher than EFA would produce — can mislead analysts into thinking they’ve found a well-defined, error-free factor, when it’s really an amalgam of all variance sources including error

Key Points (Widaman, 2007) — 2/2

  • Conversely, if the goal is just to retain variance-capturing variables but EFA is used instead of PCA: factor loadings will typically be lower than PCA — can mislead analysts into underestimating how much variance was retained
  • This distinction carries over to SEM extensions:
    • PLS-SEM — models structural relationships between component variables (PCA tradition)
    • Covariance-based SEM — models structural relationships between error-free latent variables (factor-analytic tradition)

Further Nuances — 1/2

  • The stark contrast above is a teaching simplification — most real multivariate data are correlated to some degree, so real-world PCA/EFA differences are usually less marked than a textbook example
  • As the number of variables grows, PCA and EFA results converge
    • Analyses with ≥ 40 variables tend to show only minor differences (Snook & Gorsuch, 1989)
  • High communalities (approaching 1) also bring the two methods closer together — often co-occurs with “many variables”

Further Nuances — 2/2

  • The choice of extraction method matters too: illustrative PCA-vs-EFA comparisons often use Principal Axis Factoring specifically; results can vary with other methods (e.g., ML)
  • The most important consideration — especially for psychometricians (the discipline that developed EFA) — is conceptual, not statistical:
    • EFA rests on a theory dating back to Spearman (1904): unobserved factors determine what we can measure directly
    • PCA carries no such theoretical commitment
